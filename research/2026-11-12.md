# 2026-11-12 · Why people like what they like: gloss, curves, and colour-by-association

## Focus
Research question 1 (neurology and taste), aimed at the open thread from today's profile work: Elena likes glossy, liquid, curved things (2 and 6 "drawn in water, dripping like honey"), and she feels matte colour as cold even when the hue is warm (Rex vs Robin). Do the science and her words point the same way, and what should the agent store because of it?

## Key findings
**Established (replicated or peer reviewed):**
- **Gloss is liked, possibly because it signals water.** Six studies found that adults and young children prefer glossy over matte, the preference held when people judged by touch while blindfolded, and it grew stronger when people were thirsty. The water explanation fits the data but is not proven. [Meert, Pandelaere & Patrick 2014, J. Consumer Psychology](https://core.ac.uk/works/100906670)
- **Gloss and saturation are perceived together.** When a surface looks less glossy (rougher), people see its colour as less saturated and lighter. Matte = paler and drier; gloss = deeper and "juicier". [Honson et al. 2020, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/article/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00485/full)
- **People prefer curved shapes over sharp ones.** This held for 140 pairs of real objects and for meaningless patterns. Sharp angles may read as a threat; later fMRI work from the same authors showed more amygdala activity for sharp objects. [Bar & Neta 2006, Psychological Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16913943/). There are exceptions by object type and expertise. [Carbon 2010](https://www.experimental-psychology.de/ccc/docs/pubs/Carbon2010b.pdf)
- **A colour inherits the feelings people have about things that are that colour** (the ecological valence theory). The average liking of a colour is predicted well by how much people like objects of that colour (sky and clean water make blue liked; rot makes brown disliked). It also shifts with personal identity, such as university colours. [Palmer & Schloss 2010, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2889342); [Schloss, Poggesi & Palmer 2011](https://palmerlab.berkeley.edu/pdf/SchlossPoggesi&Palmer-2011.pdf)
- **In grapheme-colour synaesthesia, shape drives colour.** Letters that look alike get similar colours, and this shape effect is about equally strong across seven languages, while language effects vary. [Brang et al. 2011](https://dare.uva.nl/id/b2d2750c-1352-4e70-8477-1c1b3ce090fd); [Root, Rouw et al. 2021](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/67032846/1_s2.0_S1053810021001185_main.pdf). Elena's own correction ("shape drives colour", 4 is purple because of its shape) matches this.
- **Senses map onto each other in everyone, not only in synaesthetes.** Colour links reliably to temperature and taste. What a shape "tastes" like can depend on the material it seems made of. [Spence 2020, temperature correspondences](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:14eb1ae8-0ac0-4821-9647-fd5a21a3975d); [Nakatani & Spence, shitsukan review](https://www.psy.ox.ac.uk/publication_modal/2426865)
- **Easy-to-process things feel pleasant** (the processing fluency account): symmetry, clear figure and ground, repetition and familiarity raise liking. Its known weak spot is explaining why challenging art is still rewarding. [Reber, Schwarz & Winkielman 2004](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~pwinkielman/reber-schwarz-winkielman-beauty-PSPR-2004.pdf)

**Speculation (mine, not from the sources):** "liquid = alive = warm" might be one chain: gloss reads as water, water reads as alive, and alive reads as warm. No paper tests that full chain.

## What this means for the agent
1. **Store material, not only colour.** Each liked or disliked item gets fields: `hue`, `finish` (gloss / satin / matte / grain), `perceived_saturation`, `temperature_word`, `taste_word`, `touch_word`, `motion_word`, plus `is` / `is_not`. Today's profile already does this in prose (Rex, Robin). Make it a schema so the open-source agent can do it for anyone.
2. **Separate the fixed hue from how it looks.** Because gloss changes how saturated a colour looks, "too cold" or "not juicy enough" can sometimes be fixed by changing the finish (adding a highlight or sheen) rather than the hex. The agent should try finish before hue when the complaint is about feel.
3. **Keep a per-person table of objects they like.** Ecological valence says a colour's appeal comes from liked objects of that colour. Ask a few times, in passing: "what's the best thing you know that is this colour?" Store the object with the colour, then predict new colours from the table.
4. **Use population priors only as a start, then let the person override them.** Curves > angles, gloss > matte and symmetry are good default guesses for a new user. Each person's confirmed signals override them; record when a prior was wrong for this person, because that difference is the interesting part.
5. **Label each liking by its likely cause**: `fluency` (easy, familiar, symmetric), `ecological` (tied to a liked object or place), `material` (gloss, softness), `identity` (team, culture, era). Different causes fade differently: fluency likes fade with exposure, while identity likes are sticky. That is useful for research question 6 (taste drift).
6. **Questions to ask in sensory language:** "Would it be better shinier or more matte, at the same colour?" "Is it wet or dry?" "If you could touch it, is it smooth in the middle or at the edges?"

## Applied to Elena
- [I] Her "warmth = juiciness" hypothesis (profile, today) fits the gloss-saturation link: Robin reads warmer partly because she imagines it glossy, and glossy colours look more saturated; Rex is matte, so it looks paler, drier and therefore colder. **Test:** show the same #FF7A45 as a matte swatch and as a glossy one with a highlight, and ask which is warmer. If the glossy Rex feels warmer, finish (not hue) is her warmth control.
- [I] Her love of 2 and 6 brings together three widely shared biases: curvature (no sharp corners), gloss (water and honey) and even divisibility, which may relate to fluency and symmetry. What's personal is the liquid, slow, honey-like motion and the specific colours, which may be ecological (red and blue objects she loves). Worth asking which objects those colours come from.

## Out-of-the-box connection of the day
**Honey ↔ thirst ↔ the "empty dome waiting for stars".** Gloss preference may come from looking for water: a shine on a surface tells a thirsty animal "something life-giving is here". Elena's night colour is an empty centre *waiting* for small lights to appear, and Rex has a smooth, calm centre with textured edges. Both may be the same pattern: a calm surface where a small bright point (a highlight, a star, a drop) is expected. Highlights on gloss are exactly that, small bright points on a smooth field. Possible single rule: *she likes calm fields that promise a small glint.* Purely speculative [I], but testable: show her a plain dark field with and without one tiny highlight.
