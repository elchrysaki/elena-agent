# 2026-11-13 · Greek vs English: two blues, direct asks, and which language carries the feeling

## Focus
Research question 2 (language), following yesterday's open thread: does Elena's Greek carry finer sensory distinctions than her English, and what changes when a Greek (and Russian) native speaker gives design feedback in English? Three angles: colour words, how requests are phrased, and which language holds emotion.

## Key findings
**Established (peer reviewed, replicated or meta-analysed):**
- **Greek has two basic blues, and Greek speakers' brains separate them before attention.** Greek splits blue into *ble* (dark) and *ghalazio* (light). In an EEG study, Greek speakers showed a larger automatic "something changed" response to light/dark blue changes than to light/dark green changes; English speakers showed no difference. [Thierry, Athanasopoulos et al. 2009, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2657373)
- **The distinction weakens with use of English.** In Greek-English bilinguals, the more available the English word "blue" was and the longer they had lived in the UK, the less they separated the two blues in similarity judgements. So the category is kept alive by using the words. [Athanasopoulos 2009, Bilingualism: Language and Cognition](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/71170/)
- **Russian does the same thing** (*siniy* dark, *goluboy* light), and Russian speakers are faster at telling blues apart across that border. [Winawer et al. 2007, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0701644104) (cited from memory, not re-checked today).
- **No language-independent ranking of the senses.** Across 20 languages, which senses are easy to put into words differs by culture. English speakers describe sight and sound easily; other languages code other senses better. Smell is weak almost everywhere. [Majid et al. 2018, PNAS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/6233065/)
- **Greek requests are more direct, and that is not rudeness.** Greek leans toward "positive politeness" (closeness, in-group warmth); a bare imperative is often not felt as an imposition at all. In call-centre openings, Greek callers were significantly more direct than British ones, explained as clarity and goal focus. [Sifianou 1992, summarised in this chapter](https://halshs.archives-ouvertes.fr/halshs-03824972v1/file/9781009123037c03_p77-102_with%20correctionsCD.pdf); [Economidou-Kogetsidis 2005](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/iprg.2005.2.3.253/html)
- **Decisions change in a foreign language.** A meta-analysis found a reliable "foreign language effect" on risk and moral choices, not explained by proficiency or age of learning. The usual explanation is reduced emotional resonance in the later-learned language; the mechanism is still debated. [Del Maschio et al. 2022](https://boa.unimib.it/retrieve/99658ada-a8e0-4a71-9d8a-fadb1ad92eaf/Del%20Maschio-2022-Bilingualism-VoR.pdf); [Circi et al. 2021 (47 experiments), via](https://munin.uit.no/handle/10037/26218)
- **Greek-English bilinguals change social code with the language.** The same story got different emotional reactions in Greek and in English, and some emotion words (Panayiotou's example is *stenahoria*) have no English twin. [Panayiotou 2004](https://gnosis.library.ucy.ac.cy/handle/7/46399?show=full) (abstract level only; details from my memory of the paper).
- **In Greek speakers' emails, English is about 20% of the words and is used as a tool of identity.** A secondary summary says affect and evaluation went mostly into English, with Greek kept for facts. I could not check that against the full text. [Tsiplakou 2009, Pragmatics](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/prag.19.3.04tsi)

**Speculation (mine):** the last two findings pull in opposite directions (native language = more feeling; English = where young Greeks put evaluation online). For a 19-year-old who works in English daily, English may be the *evaluating* language and Greek the *feeling* one. Untested.

## What this means for the agent
1. **Tag every stored utterance with its language** (`lang: el | en | ru | mixed`). A phrase decoder is per language: "blue" and "μπλε" are not the same entry.
2. **Colour words get a `native_term` field.** When a person says "blue" in English and their native language splits it, store which one they meant. One question resolves it: "μπλε ή γαλάζιο;"
3. **Calibrate directness per language background.** For a Greek speaker, a bare imperative ("push it", "make it smaller") is neutral, not annoyed. Annoyance needs a separate marker (for Elena: swearing, "bro", "come on"). Never read tone from grammar alone.
4. **Ask sensory questions in the native language when English runs out.** If the person hesitates or uses a vague English word ("vibes", "off"), offer: "say it in Greek if that's easier". Store the Greek word as the handle, with an English gloss, the same way Rex and Robin are stored.
5. **Mark emotional weight by language.** A judgement given in the native language may be closer to the gut reaction; one given in the second language may be more considered. Keep both, label them, and do not average them.
6. **Code-switch points are data.** When someone drops a Greek word into English (or the reverse), that word had no good twin. Log it as a candidate "untranslatable" with what it IS and is NOT.

## Applied to Elena
- [I] Both her native languages (Greek and Russian) split blue in two. Her profile already separates "6 = glossy lighter blue" from "velvet = very dark blue, just off black", and she rejected a blue that drifted to purple. She may be working with two different colours that English forces under one word. **Test:** ask whether 6 is γαλάζιο and velvet is μπλε, and whether they feel like relatives or strangers.
- [I] Her short, direct English ("Yes, X", "push it", "no, <list>") is Greek-style efficiency, and her real annoyance channel is separate (swearing). The profile already treats it this way; this gives the reason.
- [I] Her richest material descriptions so far (honey, cardboard grain, the dome) came in English. Greek might give even finer ones (γυαλιστερό / λαμπερό / ζουμερό / βελούδινο), or English might be her design language because she learned design in it. Worth one experiment, not an assumption.

## Out-of-the-box connection of the day
**Names keep categories alive.** Greek speakers lose the two-blue boundary when the single word "blue" takes over. Elena does the reverse on purpose: she splits one orange into Rex and Robin and gives each a name. Naming is how she stops a distinction from dissolving. For the agent, this means her named colours are not nicknames, they are her category borders, and a profile that stores only hex codes would slowly erase them, the way "blue" erases γαλάζιο.
