# 2026-11-15 · Environment and culture: taste has a season, a skyline and a training history

## Focus
Research question 4 (how surroundings, culture, era and media shape aesthetics), following the thread from the 14th: Greek built environment and light vs the Apple/Webflow references she learned design from. Four angles: does culture change *composition* (not only colour)? Does the environment change preference over weeks (season)? Does the visual environment you grew up in shape what you find pleasant? And how does training (her daily design work) reshape taste?

## Key findings
**Established (peer reviewed; replication status noted):**
- **Culture changes composition, not just colour.** East Asian landscape paintings place the horizon higher than Western ones (more context, less depth); Western portraits make the face larger, East Asian ones show more background. The same difference appears in drawings and photos by present-day students, and in children from about age 8. Mostly one lab's work; independent replication is thin. [Masuda, Gonzalez, Kwan & Nisbett 2008, PSPB](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0146167208320555); [Senzaki, Masuda & Nand 2014, Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01041/full)
- **Colour preference moves with the season, through the environment.** The same people rated colours in all four seasons: in autumn they liked dark warm colours (dark red, dark orange, dark chartreuse) more. In an 11-week autumn study, liking for leaf-matched colours rose and fell with how colourful the leaves were; non-leaf colours did not change. Explanation: ecological valence (see 2026-11-12): the objects around you "activate" their colours. [Schloss et al. 2017, Cognitive Science](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/cogs.12429); [Schloss & Heck 2017, i-Perception](https://schlosslab.discovery.wisc.edu/new-publication-seasonal-changes-in-color-preferences-are-linked-to-variations-in-environmental-colors-a-longitudinal-study-of-fall)
- **Some "environment shapes perception" classics have collapsed.** The carpentered-world hypothesis (people raised among right-angled buildings see the Müller-Lyer illusion more strongly) is now judged almost certainly false: animals fall for it too, and the old cross-cultural data have other explanations. Lesson: be careful turning "where she grew up" into a cause. [Amir & Firestone 2025, Psychological Review](https://perception.jhu.edu/files/PDFs/25_MullerLyer/AmirFirestone_MullerLyer_2025_PsychReview.pdf)
- **Some base preferences are shared and early, not local.** Adults and children from age 3 prefer low-to-moderate complexity fractal patterns (the statistics of clouds, trees, coastlines), and this did not vary with exposure to nature. Where people grew up mattered only for a narrower measure (desire to explore a scene, stronger in rural/peri-urban raised Britons). [Robles et al. 2020, Humanities & Social Sciences Communications](https://link.springer.com/10.1057/s41599-020-00648-y); [fractal preference overview, Frontiers 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.01439/pdf)
- **Daylight perception differs by region even inside Europe.** In VR daylit rooms, participants in Greece, Switzerland and Norway differed in how pleasant and calm spaces felt; Greek participants rated the same rooms brighter than Norwegians did. Facade geometry mattered more than region. VR, modest samples. [Chamilothori et al. 2022, LEUKOS, EPFL repository](https://infoscience.epfl.ch/entities/publication/b6c034ba-1f69-4ffc-8121-8977942c2de3)
- **Training changes interest more than feeling.** Art experts rate abstract works higher on beauty and "wanting" but do not differ in valence/arousal; experts treat abstract and representational art alike, novices split them sharply; a short training nudged novice preferences only slightly. Silvia's point: experts find complex art more *interesting and understandable*, which a single "liking" score hides. [van Paasschen, Bacci & Melcher 2015, PLoS ONE](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4526639/); [Bimler, Snellock & Paramei 2019](https://hira.hope.ac.uk/2664/2/Bimler%20Snellock%20Paramei_Acta%20Psychologica%202019_post-print.pdf); [Gartus, Völker & Leder 2020, Symmetry](https://mdpi.com/2073-8994/12/5/707/htm)

**Speculation (mine):** two time scales of environmental influence: a fast, reversible one (season, what is around you this month) and a slow one (training, the references you study). The fast one should be discounted; the slow one *is* the "levelling up" the agent must follow.

## What this means for the agent
1. **Stamp every preference with season and place**, not only date: `season`, `location`, `daylight` (day/night when judged, if known). Colour likes formed in one season stay [I] until they survive another season.
2. **Split "liking" into three scores** where the person gives enough signal: `like` (feels good), `interest` (wants to look longer), `fit` (right for this project). Taste growth shows up first in `interest` for complex things, before `like` follows. A rising-interest/low-like item is a future like, not a reject.
3. **Track a "reference diet".** Log which references the person studies each week (sites, skills, apps). Training effects are slow and cumulative, so a shift in taste that follows a change in diet is real growth; a shift that follows the weather is probably seasonal. This gives the agent a way to tell drift from noise.
4. **Store composition preferences as their own fields**: `figure_size` (subject dominant vs context-rich), `horizon` (high/low), `depth` (flat vs deep). These are cultural and stable, more so than colours.
5. **Do not explain taste by origin without evidence.** "She's Greek, so she likes white and blue" is the carpentered-world mistake. Origin is a hypothesis to test with one question, never a stored cause.
6. **Population priors that hold early and broadly** (moderate fractal complexity, curves, gloss) are safe defaults for a new user; regional/cultural priors are weak and should start at low weight.
7. **Questions to ask:** "Did you like this colour last spring too?"; "Is this something you'd look at longer, or something you'd actually use here?"; "What are you looking at a lot this month?"

## Out-of-the-box connection of the day
**Her signature hero is a high-context composition dressed in Western big type.** Masuda's split is figure-dominant (Western: big face, low horizon) vs context-rich (East Asian: small figure, much scene). Her repeated signature puts a giant brand word *inside the scene*, behind a subject cut from the same photo. That fuses figure and ground: the word becomes part of the landscape, and the subject sits in it. It is neither style; it may be how someone with two native languages and a third working language composes: one layer of meaning embedded in another rather than placed on top. Speculative [I]; testable by showing her the same hero with the word *over* the scene (label style) vs *in* it, and asking which feels more "hers".
