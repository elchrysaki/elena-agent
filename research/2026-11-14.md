# 2026-11-14 · Context and experience: every judgement has a neighbour, a history and a side effect

## Focus
Research question 3 (how context, memory and experience shape perception and choices), plus the thread left open on the 12th and 13th: does finish (matte vs gloss) change perceived *temperature*? The practical angle: when Elena says "love it" or "no", how much of that is the design, and how much is what she saw just before, how often she has seen it, and the fact that she already chose once?

## Key findings
**Established (peer reviewed; meta-analyses where noted):**
- **A judgement is pulled toward the one just before it.** People rating paintings one after another gave each painting a score closer to the previous painting's attractiveness. The pull is toward the previous item (assimilation), it was strongest when the two paintings were similar in attractiveness, and it held even at 250 ms exposures. [Kim, Burr & Alais 2019, Journal of Vision](https://flore.unifi.it/handle/2158/1175354); [same work in Kim's thesis](https://ses.library.usyd.edu.au/handle/2123/25395)
- **But a clearly better item makes the next one look worse, if they are seen as the same kind of thing.** Hedonic contrast: mediocre items are rated lower after very good ones, and higher after bad ones (shown for music, faces, drinks, birds). The effect shrinks or disappears when people treat the two sets as different categories. A related effect, "hedonic condensation", means the differences *between* lesser options also shrink after a great one. [Zellner, Parker et al., Beauty beyond compare](https://digitalcommons.montclair.edu/psychology-facpubs/110); [music study 2008](https://digitalcommons.montclair.edu/psychology-facpubs/378); [Cogan, Parker & Zellner 2013, faces](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/0301006616670924)
- **Repeated exposure raises liking, then lowers it.** A meta-analysis of 268 curves from 81 articles found an inverted U: liking rises with repetition, peaks, then falls. The pattern held for visual stimuli and not for sounds. I could not read the full text, so I do not have the number of exposures at the peak. [Montoya et al. 2017, Psychological Bulletin](https://www.pubmed.gov/28263645/)
- **Choosing changes what you like.** After people pick between two similar options, they like the chosen one more and the rejected one less. The classic method had a statistical flaw ([Chen & Risen critique, reviewed by Izuma & Murayama 2013](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3566335/)), but a meta-analysis of 43 studies that fixed the flaw still found a medium effect (d = 0.40, no sign of publication bias). [Enisman, Shpitzer & Kleiman 2021, JPSP](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/choice-changes-preferences-not-merely-reflects-them-a-meta-analys/)
- **People like the most novel thing that is still recognisable** ("most advanced, yet acceptable"). For product designs, typicality and novelty each predict liking about equally, and each hides the other's effect. Later replications are mixed and depend on the product category. The claim that experts weigh novelty more is plausible, but the summaries I found contradict each other, so treat it as unconfirmed. [Hekkert, Snelders & van Wieringen 2003](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/most-advanced-yet-acceptable-typicality-and-novelty-as-joint-pred/)
- **What we attach to our teens is memory more than taste.** In 470 adults aged 18 to 82, songs from around age 14 were the most familiar and the most tied to personal memories. Liking followed that bump only in people over about 40; people aged 18 to 40 did not prefer the music of their own adolescence. (An older study put the preference peak at age 23.5.) [Jakubowski et al. 2020](https://openaccess.city.ac.uk/24648/1/1747021820940300.pdf) (figures from the abstract and secondary summaries)
- **Finish and temperature (the open thread): no clean answer, and the little there is points away from Elena.** I found no study that isolates gloss vs matte on perceived thermal warmth. Wall-material studies list glossiness as a possible factor but report no agreement on its direction ([Wastiels et al., indoor wall materials](https://structurae.net/fr/litterature/article-de-revue/relating-material-experience-to-technical-parameters-a-case-study-on-visual-and-tactile-warmth-perception-of-indoor-wall-materials); from memory, that work found colour dominates and rougher surfaces read warmer). A packaging study found matte reads as *socially* warm and gloss as competent, which is a different meaning of "warm". [JPBM packaging study](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/JPBM-07-2019-2466/full/html)

**Speculation (mine):** assimilation and contrast are probably two ranges of one mechanism: a small step from the previous item gets absorbed, a big step gets exaggerated. No source I read today states it that way for design previews.

## What this means for the agent
1. **Store the neighbours with every judgement.** Each feedback record gets `seen_before` (what she looked at immediately before, and her reaction to it), `shown_with` (the alternatives on screen), and `position` (1st, 2nd, 3rd shown). A "love it" after seven rejections and a "love it" on a first showing are different pieces of evidence.
2. **Tag how the preference was produced:** `rated` (reacted to one thing), `chosen` (picked among options), `built_on` (she kept working on it). Choosing inflates liking of the pick by itself, so a `chosen` signal needs one later confirmation before it becomes a durable rule.
3. **Count exposures.** Add `times_seen` and `first_seen` to anything she keeps looking at (a hero, a colour). Rising liking in the first few views can be exposure; a "no" that arrives after many views can be the far side of the inverted U (boredom), not a flaw. Ask "is it wrong, or are you tired of it?" before redesigning.
4. **Re-test after a gap.** A preference counts as confirmed only when it survives a cold look: a different day, shown first, with nothing next to it. This is cheap: one screenshot at the start of the next session.
5. **Control the order of previews.** Never show the weakest option right after the strongest (contrast will kill it unfairly, and condensation will blur the differences between the rest). When comparing near-equals, show them side by side; when showing something bold, show it alone and first.
6. **Give each person a typicality/novelty setting per audience,** not one number. Store `structure: familiar | novel` and `details: familiar | novel` separately, because a person can want a typical skeleton with novel details.
7. **Do not treat early likes as the root of taste.** The teen "bump" is mostly memory. For a young user the agent should weight what they choose now, and keep old likes as biography (useful for metaphors and references), not as predictions.
8. **When a population finding and the person disagree, the person wins, and the disagreement is stored.** Field: `prior_conflict: true` with the source. These conflicts are the most identifying facts in the profile.

## Out-of-the-box connection of the day
**Her 1·2·3 switcher is an exposure-and-order control she invented for herself.** She asks to keep alternatives behind a per-section switcher and compares them "over time". Read against today's findings, that habit does three things research says protect a judgement: it removes order effects (she can flip in any order, so no design is always "the one after the good one"), it spreads exposure evenly (no option wins just by being seen more), and it adds the time gap that lets a choice-inflated favourite cool down. She also hates being pulled by scroll magnets. Possible single rule: *she wants to set the pace and order of her own perception, in the product and in the review process.* Speculative [I], but it predicts she will dislike any review flow that forces a sequence (a slideshow of options, "next" buttons, auto-advancing demos).
